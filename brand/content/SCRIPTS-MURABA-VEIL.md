## V1 — One apartment wide

This skyscraper is one apartment wide.

Three hundred and eighty metres tall — and just twenty-two and a half metres across. One apartment. That's the entire width of the building.

It's called Muraba Veil, and it stands where Sheikh Zayed Road crosses the Dubai Canal. Seventy-three floors — and only one hundred and thirty-one homes in the whole thing.

Because every apartment runs the full width of the tower, every single home gets both sides. The city and the sunrise on one face. The canal, the sea and the sunset on the other.

No corridor of front doors. No neighbours across the hall. On most floors, your apartment is the floor.

The press are calling it the world's thinnest residential skyscraper. Stand on Sheikh Zayed Road in a few years and from one angle it will dominate the skyline. Take a few steps to the side — and it almost disappears.

## V2 — The building that moves

This building will never look the same twice.

It's called Muraba Veil, and the name is literal. The entire tower — three hundred and eighty metres of it — is wrapped in a veil of stainless steel mesh. A modern version of the mashrabiya: the carved screens Arabic homes used for centuries to let the breeze in and keep the sun out.

Here's the part I love. The residents control it. Each home can open its section of the veil to the city — or close it, and disappear.

So the facade is alive. Panels opening and closing all day long, and the tower wearing a different face every hour.

From inside, you see everything. From outside, nobody sees you.

The developer told me this kind of moving skin has never been attempted at this height. Looking at it, I believe them.

*Cut notes: veil texture macro → panels opening in sequence; day timelapse feel; inside-looking-out vs outside-looking-in; end on facade shifting.*

## V3 — The architects who started with the sun

These architects had never built a skyscraper.

Now they're building Dubai's thinnest — and they've already won the biggest prize in architecture.

Rafael Aranda, Carme Pigem and Ramon Vilalta. Three partners from a small town in Catalonia, Spain, working together since nineteen eighty-eight. In twenty seventeen they won the Pritzker Prize — architecture's highest honour — and it was the first time in history three architects shared it.

A Dubai developer called Muraba found them long before that. Their first project together, on the Palm, finished in twenty seventeen — the same year they won. The Veil is the fifth project of a partnership that's now run ten years.

And they didn't start this tower with a shape. They started with the sun — placing the building so one solid wall blocks the hottest afternoon light, the other blocks the desert wind, and every home keeps a gentle breeze.

Most towers here are designed to be photographed. This one was designed to be lived in.

*Cut notes: three architect portraits → Pritzker moment → Palm building → sun-path diagram over the plot → wind arrows; end wide on the tower.*

## V4 — A courtyard house at three hundred and eighty metres

How did homes here stay cool before AC?

Fifty-degree summers, no air conditioning — and the answer is now shaping one of Dubai's most expensive towers.

The old courtyard house worked in three layers. A garden at its heart, with water and plants cooling the air. Shaded walkways around it, holding the cool in. And the living rooms, tucked into the protected middle.

Muraba Veil takes that idea and lifts it three hundred and eighty metres into the sky. Your living space sits in the protected centre. On either side, deep shaded terraces with gardens and ponds — the courtyard, reborn in the air. The breeze filters through, cooled by the water and the shade, and the home needs less air conditioning in the hottest city on earth.

The founder put it best: they wanted the same calm you feel when you step into the courtyard of an Arabic home.

Some ideas don't age. They just wait.

## V5 — You'll never meet your neighbours

You will never meet your neighbours here.

In most towers you share the lift with hundreds of strangers every day. In this one, you might never see another resident at all.

At Muraba Veil, a private lift opens into your own apartment's entrance hall. On most floors, your home is the entire floor — so there is no corridor, no row of front doors, nobody standing next to you holding their coffee.

The whole building is engineered around one idea: privacy. Even the arrival works that way — the developer designed it below ground, away from the heat and the street, so you come home through a quiet, protected landscape instead of a lobby full of people.

The founder told us something in that meeting that stuck with me. Most buildings are designed outwardly — for the impression they give. This one is designed inwardly — for the contentment of the people living in it.

In a city built to be seen, somebody just built a place to be unseen.

*Cut notes: lift opening straight into apartment; floor-plate graphic "one home per floor"; below-ground arrival render; founder quote as text; end on the veil closing.*

## V6 — The penthouse with no price

This penthouse doesn't have a price.

At the top of Dubai's thinnest tower, there's a home so exclusive they won't release the number.

I was in Muraba's gallery this week. The tower is one hundred and thirty-one homes, and the penthouse crowns all of them — five bedrooms, spanning the full length of the building, three hundred and eighty metres up.

They told us in that room it's around twenty-four thousand square feet. For scale: the biggest apartment they've published is fifteen thousand. This is a mansion in the sky, one apartment wide, with the city on one side and the open sea on the other.

And the price? Not released. When we asked, they just smiled.

The two bedrooms start around twelve million dirhams on current listings. The duplexes run past one hundred and twenty-nine million. The penthouse will cost whatever the right person is willing to pay.

Some homes have a price per square foot. This one has a waiting list for the number.

## V7 — The tower that refuses to advertise

This tower refuses to advertise.

Dubai launches sell out in forty-eight hours with billboards on every road. This one stays quiet on purpose — and I think it's the smartest marketing in the city.

Muraba Veil. Three hundred and eighty metres, one apartment wide, by Pritzker Prize winning architects. You'd expect it on every screen in Dubai. Instead: barely a brochure. The developer told us they keep printed material thin deliberately — they want people in the gallery, walking the materials, feeling the thing, before anyone talks numbers.

I spent two hours in that gallery this week. Films about the architecture. The actual concrete, cast in the sand colours of the plot. A conversation — not a pitch.

They told us they've sold around forty percent since launch. Quietly. Person by person.

Everyone else is shouting. The most confident seller in Dubai is whispering — and the right buyers are leaning in to hear it.

## V8 — The apartment bigger than a villa

This apartment is bigger than most villas.

Last week I stood in a seven-bedroom villa in Abu Dhabi — seven and a half thousand square feet. This week, a three-bedroom apartment that's five thousand. Not a typo.

At Muraba Veil, the three-bedroom is nearly three thousand square feet inside — plus another two thousand of private gardens and shaded terraces outside. Villa space, three hundred and eighty metres in the air.

Except — none of the villa's jobs. No roof to worry about. No garden to keep alive in August. No pool pump breaking in the heat. The building looks after the terraces, the ponds, the glass, even the moving facade — that's what the service charge pays for, and the developer is upfront that it's a premium one: around thirty-eight dirhams a square foot, anticipated.

Three-beds start around twenty-three million dirhams.

A villa gives you space and a list of problems. This gives you the space — and hands the list to someone else.
