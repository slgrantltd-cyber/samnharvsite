## P1 — The newlyweds' sofa company

Newlyweds started this company in nineteen sixty-nine.

He was twenty-six. She was twenty-two. Luigi Bestetti walked away from his family's traditional furniture business to start something of his own with Renata Pozzoli — a tiny upholstery workshop in Milan.

In the early days, by the company's own telling, Luigi delivered the sofas himself in a Fiat Five Hundred, while Renata went door to door with a briefcase full of catalogues.

Five years in, they bought land near Lake Como — in Brianza, the heart of Italy's furniture country — and built the factory that still makes every piece today.

The company is Living Divani. More than four hundred and fifty stores around the world now carry it — and it's still family. Their daughter Carola has run it since twenty twenty.

And when Dubai's quietest developer finished its first building on the Palm, this is who they flew in to furnish it.

## P2 — Luxury that refuses to shout

The most expensive sofas don't look expensive.

That's the entire philosophy of Living Divani, the Italian house whose own description of its work is two words: silent elegance. The CEO put it plainly — the message of Living Divani could not be shouted.

One designer has shaped that quiet for nearly four decades. Piero Lissoni asked to work with the founders in the eighties — and has been the house's art director ever since. Low lines. Perfect proportions. Furniture you don't notice until you sit down — and then you refuse to get up.

I sat on their sofas this week — in the sales gallery of Muraba Veil in Dubai. The same house that furnished Muraba's first building on the Palm, in the gallery of a tower built entirely around calm.

Loud furniture impresses your guests. Quiet furniture is for the person who lives there.

Some brands sell the room. This one sells the silence.

## P3 — The heir who chose architecture

He could have just been rich.

Ibrahim Al Ghurair comes from one of Dubai's oldest merchant dynasties — a family in business since the days of the pearl trade, the family behind Mashreq, one of the UAE's oldest banks, and the UAE's first flour mill.

He chose buildings instead. In twenty fourteen he founded Muraba — not to build towers everywhere, but to build rarely, and properly.

He's told the story of travelling the world to find his architect. He interviewed firms in London and found nothing. Then he walked into a century-old foundry in a small Spanish town, and the architects there asked him one question about his site on the Palm: where is the light coming from? That was it.

Ten years, five projects — including a school his family donated to its own neighbourhood.

His line says everything: property is not an end in itself — it's the means to a more noble one.

## P4 — Three architects from a volcano town

They won architecture's biggest prize from a volcano town.

Olot, Catalonia. Thirty-four thousand people, sitting inside a natural park with around forty volcanic cones. In nineteen eighty-eight, three architecture graduates — Rafael Aranda, Carme Pigem and Ramon Vilalta — finished their studies and simply went home. They never left.

Their studio is a century-old converted foundry. Within a year, they became the official architects of the volcanic park itself. For decades they built small and perfect — a museum in France, a kindergarten, the marquee for the two-Michelin-star restaurant in their own town — out of weathering steel that rusts into the landscape.

In twenty seventeen, the Pritzker Prize — architecture's highest honour — went to all three of them. The first trio in its history.

Now a studio that spent nearly forty years on low buildings in the Catalan countryside is building a three-hundred-and-eighty-metre tower in Dubai.

They never chased the world. It drove to Olot.
